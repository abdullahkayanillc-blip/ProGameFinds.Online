Streaming Microphones: Pickup Patterns, Placement and Gain

A microphone's headline sample rate or bit depth cannot fix poor placement or an untreated room. Clear voice audio starts with understanding where the microphone listens, how close it is to the speaker and how its input level is set.

ProGameFinds Editorial TeamPublished 28 November 20267 min read
Main idea: Choose a pickup pattern that suits the room, place the microphone consistently and set gain so loud speech remains clear without clipping. These basics often matter more than a long list of marketing numbers.

What a pickup pattern describes

A microphone's polar pattern describes how sensitive it is to sound arriving from different directions. It is usually shown as a diagram around the microphone. The shape helps explain which sounds are captured most strongly and which directions are less sensitive.

Omnidirectional

An omnidirectional microphone picks up sound from a broad range of directions. This can be useful when more than one person needs to be heard or when a natural sense of the room is wanted. It also means the microphone may capture more room ambience, keyboard noise or nearby conversations. It is not automatically the best choice for a single-person desk setup.

Cardioid and other directional patterns

A cardioid microphone is most sensitive toward its front and less sensitive toward its rear. This can help a single speaker be more prominent than a computer fan or other sound behind the microphone, provided the mic is aimed and positioned correctly. Supercardioid and hypercardioid patterns have narrower front pickup regions and different rear sensitivity. Their placement needs more care.

A directional pattern does not make a microphone ignore every sound outside its front. Reflections, room acoustics, microphone distance and sound level all affect what gets recorded. Shure's pattern guide explains the differences between these common pickup patterns.

Distance and angle can change the sound

Moving a microphone closer generally increases the voice level relative to many room sounds. Moving it too close can make plosive bursts, breath noise and bass emphasis more noticeable. Moving it too far away often makes the room and desktop environment more prominent when the recording level is raised.

Try a consistent position slightly off the direct path of strong breath sounds, then listen back with headphones. A pop filter or foam windscreen can reduce bursts of air reaching the capsule, but it does not replace sensible distance and angle. Keep the microphone away from direct contact with a desk that carries keyboard taps or mouse movement.

Gain: set the input level, not the volume of the whole stream

Input gain controls how strongly the microphone signal is amplified at the recording input. If gain is too low, the voice may be quiet compared with background noise and need more amplification later. If it is too high, loud speech can exceed the input's available range and clip. Clipping distorts the signal and cannot be fully repaired by turning down the volume afterwards.

Use an input meter while speaking at the loudest level you expect during a session. Leave headroom rather than aiming for the meter to hit its maximum. Shure's guidance explains that a signal that clips at the input needs less gain or a lower input level; watch the meter while testing normal and excited speech.

USB and XLR are connection choices, not sound-quality guarantees

A USB microphone includes a digital audio interface in the microphone or its cable assembly. It may be simple to connect to a computer, but you still need to confirm operating-system support, monitoring options and whether the device offers the controls you need.

An XLR microphone normally connects to an audio interface or mixer. That setup adds flexibility for routing and hardware control, but it also adds components and setup choices. The connector alone does not guarantee better sound. The capsule, preamp, room, placement, settings and recording workflow all contribute.

A repeatable streaming audio check

  • Choose a pickup pattern that matches the room and number of speakers.
  • Position the microphone consistently and keep it stable during a session.
  • Listen for room echo, keyboard noise, fan noise, plosives and handling noise.
  • Set gain while speaking at the loudest expected level, leaving headroom.
  • Record a short sample and listen back on headphones before going live.
  • Check monitoring delay and mute controls in the actual streaming software.

Noise gates and filters are finishing tools

Software processing can help manage room noise, but aggressive gates may cut off the beginning or end of words. Equalisation and compression can shape a voice, but they cannot recreate missing detail or remove every reflection from a poor room. Start with a clean raw recording, then add only the processing that solves an audible problem.

The practical takeaway

For clear streaming audio, prioritise a suitable pickup pattern, stable placement, controlled room noise and correct input gain. Compare microphones after these basics are consistent. Otherwise, a change in mic position or level can be mistaken for a difference in microphone quality.
