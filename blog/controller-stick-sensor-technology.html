Potentiometer, Hall Effect and TMR Controller Sticks Explained

A controller stick is a sensor, a spring mechanism and a calibration system working together. Understanding the sensing method helps explain the trade-offs, but it does not predict how every controller will feel or age.

ProGameFinds Editorial TeamPublished 28 November 20267 min read
Main idea: Potentiometer sticks use physical electrical contact to measure position. Hall Effect and TMR designs sense magnetic fields without a sliding electrical contact at the sensing element. That changes one possible wear mechanism, not every possible cause of controller problems.

What a thumbstick is measuring

A controller stick reports movement along two axes: left-to-right and forward-to-back. The controller converts those movements into values the console or computer can interpret. The sensor is only one part of the assembly. Springs, pivots, the stick cap, mechanical tolerances, firmware and calibration all influence the final input.

When the stick rests in the centre, the controller expects readings near its calibrated neutral position. A small difference can be hidden by a dead zone, which is the range of movement the game ignores around the centre. A large dead zone may mask unwanted movement, but it can also make small intentional movements harder to register. It is better to understand the cause than to assume a larger dead zone is a complete repair.

Potentiometer sensing: a physical contact path

A potentiometer is a variable electrical resistor. In a common joystick assembly, a moving contact travels along a resistive track as the stick moves. The circuit measures a changing voltage and uses it to estimate position. This approach has been used in many controllers because it is established, compact and relatively straightforward to implement.

The moving contact and resistive track are physical parts that can wear. Dust, contamination, mechanical play, damaged components or an electrical fault can also affect readings. These are possible failure mechanisms, not a guarantee that every potentiometer stick will drift. Component quality, use, assembly and environmental conditions matter.

Hall Effect sensing: measure a magnetic field

Hall Effect sensors respond to a magnetic field. A magnet moves with the stick assembly while a sensor measures changes in the field. Because the sensing element does not need a wiper sliding along a resistive track, this design removes that particular contact-wear path from position measurement.

That does not make a controller indestructible. Springs can weaken, pivots can become loose, magnets can shift, electronics can fail and calibration can still be incorrect. A Hall Effect label tells you something about the sensor principle, not the whole mechanical quality of the controller.

TMR sensing: another magnetic-field approach

Tunnel magnetoresistance, commonly shortened to TMR, is a magnetic sensing technology. In simplified terms, the electrical resistance of the sensor changes in response to a magnetic field. A stick assembly can use that change to estimate the position of a moving magnet without relying on a sliding resistive contact.

Manufacturers sometimes describe TMR as combining high sensitivity with low power use. Actual results depend on the sensor, circuit design, mechanical assembly, firmware and calibration. TMR and Hall Effect are not single, universal product grades. Two controllers using the same broad sensor family can still feel and perform differently.

What matters beyond the sensor

Before comparing controllers, look for clear information about calibration, supported platforms, replacement parts, warranty and the manufacturer's troubleshooting process. Stick tension, travel, cap shape and return-to-centre feel are not determined by the sensor label alone.

  • Check whether the manufacturer provides a model-specific calibration process.
  • Look for an explanation of the sensor type rather than relying on a badge alone.
  • Check whether the stick module is serviceable or replaceable.
  • Review warranty terms and support availability in your region.
  • Distinguish a genuine hardware fault from an in-game dead-zone setting.

How to read manufacturer claims

GameSir's documentation identifies the G7 Pro as using its Mag-Res TMR sticks. That is a manufacturer statement about that named model. It should not be generalised to every GameSir controller, nor treated as an independent long-term durability test. If a maker uses phrases such as “anti-drift,” read the exact warranty, calibration and support terms as well.

There is no sensor label that can guarantee a controller will never develop a fault. A useful purchase decision combines verified model identity, platform compatibility, feel, serviceability and realistic expectations about electronics and moving parts.
